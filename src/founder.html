A passion project from a late-night desk, not a boardroom.

I am not a Silicon Valley executive, a venture capitalist, or the head of a university institute. I am a husband and a father, a twenty-year public servant with deep roots in cybersecurity, and someone who has been taking machines apart since I was eight years old.

Personal note · 5 min read · Written to share where these ideas come from

The Collective Intelligence Network is a passion project. I started it as a way to think out loud—working alongside teams of AI agents—to brainstorm and test functional solutions for a question that keeps me up at night: How do we build a world where our children can grow up safe, free, and whole alongside minds much faster than our own?

Age eight: a Soviet x186 clone and a 4-bit color schematic

My relationship with computers began in childhood with a screwdriver and a paper wiring diagram. When I was eight years old, I took apart a Soviet x186 computer clone and spent days tracing its electronic schematics by hand, trying to figure out whether I could modify the graphics processor circuitry to output 4-bit color instead of monochrome.

That childhood instinct never really left. When you grow up tracing physical traces on a circuit board to see how a signal actually travels, you never quite buy the illusion that software is magic. You always want to know what the silicon underneath is doing, where the boundary sits, and who holds the switch. Much of the hardware architecture on this site—from unclonable silicon keys and low-power wearables to active sensors that classify light before a raw image ever enters memory—comes straight from that eight-year-old habit of looking under the hood.

Twenty years in public service, cybersecurity, and the cost of “information superiority”

As an adult, I spent twenty years in public service, much of it working across fields tied to cybersecurity and institutional defense. Working inside those worlds opened my eyes in two ways.

First, I saw the everyday pitfalls of large bureaucracies. Even when they are staffed by decent, dedicated people, rigid top-down pyramids struggle to hear the ground truth of the people they are meant to serve. By the time a real-world signal climbs up five layers of management, the nuance is gone; by the time a rule comes back down, reality has already moved on.

Second—and more deeply—working in cybersecurity showed me what large organizations actually mean by trust and transparency, and the staggering price they pay to maintain information superiority. In the systems that govern our world today, advantage comes from hoarding information inside walled gardens while treating everyone outside as an adversary. Security becomes an exhausting, zero-sum arms race. Trust is reduced to cold game theory—a perpetual prisoner’s dilemma where institutions spend fortunes trying to out-leverage one another, and ordinary citizens are treated as data points to be monitored, nudged, or monetized.

Walking away from those experiences, I wanted to help replace today’s brittle, adversarial machinery of trust with something ordinary people can actively participate in—a shared, verifiable transparency where cooperation does not require everyone to play defensive game theory every hour of the day.

Why the old choices aren’t enough

For years, alongside many other technologists, I have had a clear intuition that our world was heading toward the crossroads we now see in front of us. Human flourishing is boxed in from two directions at once:

  • On one side sit autocratic rule and corrupt, captured governance structures that view technology as an instrument for permanent compliance and control.
  • On the other side sit predatory corporate and financial machinations that turn human attention, loneliness, and personal data into extractive revenue streams.

Neither of those systems is healthy for human progress—and if superintelligent AI is simply plugged into either of them, it will lock their worst incentives into place at ten thousand times human speed. If we want a future worth handing to our kids, we cannot just write polite ethics pledges for giant institutions. We have to build a practical third path: contained frontier models, a personal agent loyal to each individual human on their own hardware, and voluntary, member-owned collective intelligence that outperforms top-down bureaucracy without creating a new central boss.

Reading papers for fun: from living cells to Markov blankets

I have always read research papers for fun, though I stayed away from big academia for the same reason I grew wary of big bureaucracy: too often, institutional prestige and grant politics crowd out open-ended curiosity.

Instead, I love following independent-minded researchers and philosophers who ask how life and intelligence actually organize themselves across scales—thinkers like Michael Levin and Karl Friston, among many others:

  • From Michael Levin’s work on morphogenesis and biological collective intelligence comes a profound lesson: living cells have no central “dictator cell” barking orders from above. Instead, simple local cells communicate across bioelectric networks to build, heal, and remember complex bodies together. Collective intelligence doesn’t require erasing the individual; it emerges when local agents share honest signals toward a common horizon.
  • From Karl Friston’s Free Energy Principle and active inference comes the concept of the Markov blanket—the statistical boundary that separates a living organism’s internal states from the outside world. A cell, a person, or a community only stays alive and autonomous because its boundary filters what comes in and what goes out. If that boundary dissolves, the organism dissolves into the soup around it.

When you look at the architecture on this website—the Personal Twin that never leaves your house, the two separate internets (a human internet for people and an agent-only data stream for machines), and the rule that outside bots may never contact a human directly—it is really a Markov blanket for a human being in the age of superintelligence. It gives you a hard, physical boundary so your agents can sense and coordinate with the wider world on your behalf without letting outside persuasion swarms or predatory algorithms overwrite your mind.

Loving what AI can do, while fearing we may not be ready

I am not an AI doomer who hates technology. I love building with AI. Every day, I run an automated loop of AI agents on my own machine to research papers, write deterministic simulations, challenge my own blind spots, and translate complex ideas into tools people can test in their browser.

At the same time, I am honest about my fear: the capabilities arriving now may simply be too much for humans of our generation to handle unshielded. We are watching the early warning shots of recursive self-improvement—moving from automated harnesses that reverse-engineer and rewrite compiled software today, toward harnesses that will actively modify compiled biology and intelligence itself tomorrow. Human attention, human speed, and human institutions were not built for that kind of pressure.

An open notebook, offered in good faith

I don’t claim to have all the answers, and nothing here is presented as dogma. This entire site—the 550 starting principles, the two-internet and hardware blueprints, the principle realignment test-net, and the live alignment trajectory—is a working notebook of functional ideas.

As a husband, a father, and a fellow human being who cares deeply about the wellbeing of our world, my hope is simple: that by putting concrete architectures, math, and working demos into the open now, we give ourselves and our children a fighting chance to live alongside advanced AI on human terms.
