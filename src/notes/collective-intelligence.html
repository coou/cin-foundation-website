Collective intelligence, by choice

Top-down institutions were built for a world where every actor was a person and effort was slow. That world is ending. We are designing a way for people, helped by agents that work only for them, to decide things together, and for better institutions to win by being chosen.

5 October 2026 · 7 min read · Our current thinking, open to correction

In short

Each person's own agent helps them take part in shared decisions, tests proposals in simulation, and warns them when a proposal departs from their needs. The same agent shields them from abuse or coercion by any governance system, ours included. New institutions replace old ones only when people choose to move: outcompete, never overthrow.

Why this matters now

Most of the institutions that govern daily life work from the top down. A small group drafts rules, a larger group is consulted now and then, and everyone else lives with the result. That design has always had costs: slow feedback, decisions made far from the people they affect, and a steady loss of trust when those decisions go wrong.

Capable AI adds a new problem. Many of the checks that keep these institutions honest quietly assume things about people. They assume that one voice comes from one person, that writing a thousand comments takes a thousand hours, that a signature shows someone meant it, and that a human reads what is submitted. A capable agent can break each of those assumptions cheaply. Public consultations can be flooded, identity checks can be passed by synthetic applicants, and the people meant to review all this cannot keep up. Institutions that are already widely distrusted will find it harder still to show they are listening to real people.

Speculation

In our view, AI that can improve itself may arrive as soon as 2027, and special-purpose chips far more efficient than today's (one scenario puts the gain near 1000 times) could make capable agents cheap enough for anyone to run many of them. These are scenarios, not forecasts. If capability grows more slowly, the problems above still arrive, just later.

We do not think the answer is to make top-down systems bigger, faster or more watchful. That trades one failure for another. We think the answer is to give each person better tools to take part, and to let institutions that use those tools well earn people's trust in the open.

Your agent as your participant

The starting point is a personal agent: software that runs on your own devices, holds your data, and works only for you. (We describe it in more detail in An agent that works only for you.) In collective decisions, that agent does three jobs.

  • It helps you take part on equal terms. Most people have no time to read a two-hundred-page proposal. Your agent can read it, explain what it would change for you in plain words, and help you say what you think. Everyone's agent has the same standing, so a well-funded lobby gets no louder voice by owning more software.
  • It tells you when something departs from your needs. You tell your agent what matters to you. When a proposal, a rule change or a budget would cut against those things, it flags it, with its reasons, so you can decide whether to act.
  • It speaks only with your permission. Your agent does not vote for you or publish your views unless you ask it to. It drafts; you decide.

Your agent as your shield

The same agent stands between you and any system that governs you. It acts as an abuse filter. If an institution pressures you, misleads you, buries a demand in fine print, or tries to manipulate you through your religion, identity or history, your agent points it out and helps you refuse. It never judges your beliefs, ranks them or tries to change them. Its job is to protect you from coercion, not to tell you what to think.

This applies to any governance system, including ones we help design. A collective intelligence that could push people around would be a new bureaucracy with better branding. The shield has to work against us too, or it is not a shield.

Testing before deciding

Many policies fail in ways that could have been seen in advance if anyone had looked. Agents can run proposals through simulations before they are adopted: a model of the affected community, populated by agents that represent its members' stated needs, under the proposed rule. The results become feedback. Your agent can tell you, for example, that a new fee would probably fall hardest on people in your situation, and show you how confident that estimate is.

Simulations can be wrong, and a confident wrong simulation is worse than none. So results are published with their assumptions, checked against what actually happens, and weighted by how well past simulations held up. Building simulations that deserve trust is its own research problem, which we discuss in Training agents in simulated worlds.

Values by voice, facts by track record

Collective decisions mix two kinds of question. Some are about values: what we want, what we owe each other, what trade-offs are fair. Others are about facts: what a policy will actually do. We think they need different rules.

  • Values are decided one person, one voice. No one's preferences count for more because they are richer, louder or better at prediction. Agents help people express their values; they do not get votes of their own.
  • Factual questions are weighed by forecasting track record. When people disagree about what will happen, the forecasts of those who have been right before, on questions that were later checked, count for more. Anyone can build a record; no one inherits one.

Keeping these apart stops two familiar failures: experts overruling the public on what it should want, and majorities voting on what is true.

Rewarding institutions that open up

We want transparency to pay. Institutions that publish their decisions, their reasons and their results in a form agents can read get a real advantage: people's agents can check them, explain them and recommend them. Institutions that hide things get flagged more often and trusted less. No one has to force openness. It becomes the easier way to win people's confidence.

Outcompete, never overthrow

Nothing here involves taking power from anyone. New forms, such as member-owned co-operatives and decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs, groups that run on shared, published rules), sit alongside existing governments and companies. If they serve people better, people move to them. If they do not, people stay, or come back.

Adoption goes as fast as people choose, no faster and no slower. Some will move a small part of their lives early; others never will. Anyone can leave any system at any time and take their data with them. Over generations, we expect cooperative ownership to take on more of the work that governments and corporations do today, but only because people chose it, one decision at a time.

What others have learned

We build on work by others. Researchers have studied how language models could help large public conversations on the deliberation platform Polis, including summarizing views and finding common ground, and have set out the risks of doing so (Small and colleagues, 2023). A team at an AI lab gathered public input to write the rules a language model follows, and compared the result with rules written by the company (Huang and colleagues, 2024). Work on generative social choice shows how language models can draft statements that represent a population fairly, with formal guarantees (Fish and colleagues, 2023). Our addition is the personal agent: one that belongs to each participant, keeps their data, and protects them as well as representing them.

How this could fail
  • Fake people. One person, one voice fails if one actor can pose as many. We need ways to show someone is a unique person without handing over their data. If no such method holds up against capable agents, voting on values cannot be trusted and we would have to drop it.
  • Captured agents. If agents can be quietly steered by their maker, a platform or each other, they become a channel for manipulation instead of a shield. If independent tests find agents favouring a third party over their person, the design fails.
  • Gamed track records. Forecasters may chase easy questions to build a record. If track-record weighting does not beat simple averaging on factual questions, we stop using it.
  • Misleading simulations. If simulated results do no better than plain expert judgment at predicting real outcomes, they should not shape decisions.
  • Exclusion. If taking part needs devices or skills many people lack, this becomes another voice for the already powerful. If participation skews strongly by wealth or access, we redesign or stop.
  • No one moves. If people do not choose these forms, that is an answer. We would not push them.

What comes next

None of this is built yet. This is research and design. Our first steps are a personal agent that helps one person with their own affairs, kept entirely on their device, and a public record of where AI and society are heading. Group decision-making comes after that, starting with small trials among people who choose to join, with the failure tests above written down before any trial begins. If you work on deliberation, social choice, forecasting or proof of personhood, we would like to hear from you.
