Two internets: one for people, one for agents

The web was built for people reading pages at human speed. Agents that act at machine speed need their own doors, with identity, limits and records, so that the human web stays human.

5 October 2026, updated 14 October · 8 min read · Our current thinking, open to correction

In short

We propose that services meet automated agents through serving agents of their own, not through open APIs or by letting bots pose as people. Your agent holds your credentials and proves only the single fact a service needs. People keep a separate, human-paced internet that never has to compete with machine traffic. Why this matters

Online trust rests on the quiet assumption that one form, one review, or one queue entry comes from one person at human speed—an assumption that collapses as soon as capable software agents act cheaply in large numbers.

An agent can fill in ten thousand forms, write a million reviews, book every appointment slot in a city in a second, or flood a public consultation with plausible comments. When that traffic arrives through the same doors people use, people lose.

The usual answers are to put up more barriers against bots, which also burden people, or to open more APIs, which hand raw data and broad powers to any program holding a key. A third path is to give agents and people separate spaces, each with rules that suit it.

Speculation

Our working assumption is that agents keep getting more capable, cheaper and more autonomous, and that AI able to improve itself may arrive as soon as 2027.

How it would work: Serving agents, single-fact proofs & two doors

Instead of open APIs, a clinic, shop, or public office runs a serving agent that negotiates with your personal client agent (Agora protocol), verifying AI system identifiers and authenticated delegation one fact at a time.

1. Services answer through serving agents, not open APIs

The serving agent publishes what questions it will answer, refuses requests outside its published terms, and logs what it did—never seeing more than a query needs.

2. Your agent holds your credentials and proves one fact at a time

When a service needs to know you are over 18, a licensed electrician or a resident of a city, your agent proves that one fact and nothing else, tracking disclosure budgets so small answers cannot be combined into a profile.

3. Separate doors, and neither side competes with the other

People never queue behind machine-speed traffic, and agents get a door built for them so they have no reason to pose as people.

4. Robots act only on a mandate, with limits outside the model

Physical limits on speed, force, and geofences are enforced by hardware limit switches outside the AI model. Fully independent embodied agents answering to no one are never deployed.

5. What an agent would carry

  • Whom it acts for: Accountable single-person or community credential without identity exposure.
  • What shapes its answers: Declared model family, priorities, and blind spots.
  • Its limits & records: Expiring permissions, compute budgets, and bilaterally signed receipts across circles of trust and liveness checks.

Why this keeps AI safe—and how it could fail

Splitting the internet enforces decentralized principal-bound multi-agent alignment: no agent gets raw database access, every action has a human owner, and frontier models stay behind logged verifier layers.

How this could fail
  • Identity becomes a choke point: We favour many independent issuers, zero-knowledge single-fact checks, and no single registry.
  • One person, a thousand agents: Requires hardware-rooted rate limits without invasive surveillance.
  • Agents slip through the human door: Addressed via local pre-ADC somatic presence proofs.
  • Small answers add up: Tested against synthetic profile-reconstruction attacks.
  • Exclusion & uptake: The human door must remain a first-class service, never a neglected fallback.
