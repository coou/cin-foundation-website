Quests, not feeds

When machines can produce unlimited content, the scarce thing is knowing what matters to you right now. We think your own agent should answer that question, in a small window you control, and never for anyone else's benefit.

5 October 2026 · 7 min read · Our current thinking, open to correction

In short

Instead of searching, or wading through a flood of AI-generated material, you would see a short list of "quests": the few things that matter to you now, chosen by an agent that works only for you. You decide whether to use it and what it learns from, you can always see why something is shown, and it is never designed to hold your attention.

Why this matters

For most of history, information was scarce and finding it was the hard part. Search engines solved much of that. Then feeds arrived, and the problem flipped: there was too much, and the ranking was done by companies paid for your attention. Generative AI pushes this further. Text, images, video and messages can now be produced at almost no cost, in any volume, tuned to whoever is watching. A growing share of what reaches you will be written by machines, and some of it will be written by machines trying to persuade you.

In that world, the useful question is not "where can I find more?" but "what, out of all this, actually needs me?" A bill that is about to go up. A form with a deadline. A message from someone you care about that got buried. A decision that will be much harder next month than today. These are easy to miss, not because they are hidden, but because they are surrounded.

People already rely on filters to cope: friends, editors, assistants, habits. The trouble with today's main filters is whom they serve. A feed ranks by what keeps you looking, because that is what pays. We want a filter whose only loyalty is to the person using it.

The idea: a system window

Some games and stories use a simple device. The player sees a small window, a "system" panel, that lists what is happening and what they might do next: a handful of quests, each with a reason and a next step. Nothing scrolls forever. The window is there when you want it and quiet when you don't.

We think a personal interface could work the same way. Your agent, which runs for you and keeps your data on your own device, looks at what it is allowed to see: your accounts, your messages, your calendar, answers from other services and agents. It picks out the few items that matter and shows them as quests. Each quest says what it is, why it is shown, and what the smallest useful next step would be.

How it would work

The design rests on a few commitments. Each one closes off a way that today's feeds go wrong.

  • Strictly opt-in, and revocable. The quest window is off until you turn it on. You choose which sources it may read, and you can withdraw any of them, or turn the whole thing off, at any time, without losing anything else your agent does for you.
  • You can see why. Every quest carries its reason in plain words: "your phone bill rose 18% this month", "this deadline is in four days", "you asked to hear from this person". If the agent cannot give a reason, it should not show the item. Numbers, dates and matches come from ordinary, checkable code, not from a language model guessing.
  • Few items, then quiet. The window shows a small set and then stops. Finishing it is the goal. There is no endless scroll and no "one more thing" to keep you there.
  • No engagement tricks. No streaks, no badges for logging in, no counts of time spent, no notifications designed to pull you back. The agent is not measured on how long you look at it. If anything, it succeeds when you need it less.
  • Tuned by feedback you choose to give. You can tell it "more like this", "not now" or "never this". You can also, if you want, tell it how something made you feel: that a reminder was a relief, or that a topic left you anxious. Nobody has to give feedback, and the window still works without it.
  • Feelings never leave your device. Emotional signals are the most intimate data there is. Whatever you share about how you feel is used only by your own agent, on your own device. It is never sent to a remote model, never sold and never pooled.

The agent behind the window is the same personal agent described in our other notes: a bounded assistant that keeps your data with you and answers only to you. When it needs something from a service, it asks a specific question and gets a specific answer or proof back, rather than handing over your records. The quest window is simply the place where that work becomes visible to you.

What a quest is, and is not

A quest is a suggestion, not an order. It can say "this charge looks like a subscription you stopped using; here is how to cancel it", but it cannot cancel anything unless you have explicitly allowed that kind of action. It can surface a message you would want to see, but it does not decide what you believe about it.

That line matters most on questions of value and belief. Your agent can warn you when something looks like coercion or manipulation, for example a message that uses your religion, identity or history to pressure you into paying or acting. It does not judge, rank or try to change what you believe, and it does not nudge you toward anyone's preferred opinion, including ours.

Speculation

Today the window would be a screen: a phone, a laptop, perhaps glasses. Further out, the same idea could reach people through voice, wearables or brain-computer interfaces, which some groups are already researching. We think the principles should not change with the device: opt-in, visible reasons, nothing that optimizes for attention, and inner states that never leave the person. Interfaces that read signals directly from the body or brain would raise the stakes sharply, and we would expect stricter limits there, not looser ones. This is a long-term direction, not a plan with dates, and it could prove unwise or unworkable.

How this could fail

How this could fail
  • It becomes a feed again. Any ranked list can drift toward whatever gets clicked. If we find the window being tuned, by us or anyone else, toward time spent or return visits, the design has failed and we would drop it.
  • Emotional data becomes surveillance. If feelings cannot in practice be kept on the device, or if people feel they must share them to get a useful service, we would remove emotional feedback rather than weaken the protection.
  • A quiet filter bubble. Learning from your reactions can hide things you need but would rather not see. The window must keep showing what is important even when it is unwelcome, and let you inspect and reset what it has learned.
  • Capture by whoever pays. If advertisers, platforms or governments can buy or force placement in the window, it no longer works for you. Paid or mandated placement would end the project in its current form.
  • Manipulation of the agent itself. As AI systems grow more capable, other agents will try to get their content marked as urgent. Reasons must be checkable, and sources that game the window should lose its trust.
  • It does not help. If people who try it do not find that they miss fewer important things, or feel no less overwhelmed, then it is not worth building.

What comes next

Nothing here is built yet. This is research and design. The first step is modest: a personal assistant that runs on your own device and handles one narrow job, such as finding recurring charges in your own bank statements and explaining them. That is a natural first source of quests, because the reasons are concrete and the numbers can be checked.

From there, the questions we want to work on are practical ones. How few items are enough? How should reasons be phrased so people can actually check them? How can someone see and reset what the agent has learned about them? How do we test whether the window reduces overload without measuring people in ways that would themselves be intrusive?

If you work on calm technology, interface design, recommender ethics or on-device AI, and you see a flaw in this approach, we would like to hear about it.
