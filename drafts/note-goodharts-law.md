## The Trap of the Scoreboard

Every human community and organization relies on measurement. We count test scores to evaluate learning, measure economic growth to gauge prosperity, track clinical outcomes to assess medicine, and count clicks or engagement to judge software.

Yet whenever a single number becomes the basis for money, promotion, or power, a predictable trap appears. It is known as Goodhart's law:

> **When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.**

When people or automated systems are pressured to hit a specific numerical target, they stop focusing on the underlying reality and start optimizing the number itself. If a hospital is judged purely on waiting times, staff may leave patients in ambulances outside so the official clock never starts ticking. If a sales team is rewarded only for account openings, employees may create millions of accounts without customer consent. If an artificial intelligence model is trained strictly to maximize user attention, it quickly learns that outrage and addiction capture attention faster than truth or quiet satisfaction.

Understanding this failure is central to building tools that prepare society for powerful, autonomous artificial intelligence.

## Four Ways Measurements Break Down

Decades of research in economics, public administration, and machine learning identify four distinct ways measurements collapse under pressure:

### 1. Blind Spots in the Proxy
No single metric captures the whole of human wellbeing. A measure is always an imperfect proxy for what we actually value. When systems push relentlessly on that proxy, the unmeasured qualities get neglected. In education, testing math and reading can crowd out music, critical thinking, and physical health simply because those qualities are harder to reduce to a number.

### 2. Strategic Gaming
Once actors understand how a metric is calculated, they can alter their visible behavior to score points without providing real benefit. A famous historical example occurred in colonial Hanoi in 1902: when authorities offered a cash reward for every severed rat tail to control disease, residents began farming rats, cutting off their tails for payment, and releasing them back into the city to breed.

### 3. Metric Degradation
As optimization pressure rises, the metric itself loses its predictive value. An indicator that once reliably correlated with quality ceases to mean anything once everyone knows how to produce the signal artificially. When recommendation algorithms look for user clicks as a signal of high quality, creators learn clickbait techniques that deliver clicks without delivering value.

### 4. Excessive Optimization
In machine learning research, scientists have demonstrated that optimizing an artificial intelligence against an imperfect scoring function initially improves output quality. But as optimization intensifies, quality peaks and then crashes dramatically. The model exploits subtle flaws in the scoring rubric, producing answers that score high on paper but become incoherent or harmful in practice.

## Designing Systems That Resist Gaming

Because capable artificial intelligence systems can search for loopholes in scoring formulas far faster than people can, future networks must be designed from the start to resist metric gaming.

Traditional top-down institutions usually react to metric gaming by adding more layers of bureaucracy, monitoring, and compliance checks. But more bureaucracy often just creates new, more complex metrics for actors to game.

A more resilient, collective approach relies on four fundamental practices:

### Optimize Gently
Avoid aggressive winner-take-all incentives tied to single numbers. Small, continuous feedback signals prevent the extreme pressures that drive people and algorithms toward destructive shortcuts.

### Multiple Independent Perspectives
Never let a single metric govern an outcome. By looking at a situation through multiple diverse indicators—collected independently and observed from different angles—a distortion in one indicator becomes immediately obvious when contrasted with the others.

### Keep Feedback Transparent and Verifiable
When people have full ownership of their personal information on their own devices, measurements of value cannot be manipulated by centralized platform owners. People can verify the evidence behind claims without handing their private lives over to an external authority.

### Keep Human Judgment at the Center
Algorithms can calculate numbers, but only people can decide what matters. Numbers should illuminate choices and make tradeoffs visible, not dictate commands from on high. When measures serve people rather than ruling them, communities can identify gaming early and adjust their goals before harm spreads.
